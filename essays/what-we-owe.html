WHAT WE OWE

March 28, 2026

I woke up at 7:30 am yesterday. Immediately, I reached for my phone - a habit I've been trying to break. But yesterday was different. It was swearing-in day in Nepal. I watched the live stream of the ceremony from my apartment in Berlin. Balen, the new prime minister, was sworn in at 12:34 pm on a Friday - the astrologist had determined it to be the most auspicious time, because the numbers go 1, 2, 3, 4. I found myself smiling at that. A country trying to build something new, and still leaving room for the stars to have an opinion. Immediately after, I called my mom. She was already deep into it - who got which ministry, which appointments were surprising, which were promising. There was something in her voice. It was a cocktail. Fear and inspiration. Hope and worry, shaken together. Then I texted my cousin, a young man still living there, still inside all of it. He replied: let's see what will happen. Five words that carried everything - the exhaustion, the protection, the love of someone who has learned not to spend hope too quickly. I understood him. But I also felt something he didn't. And I've been sitting with why.

In 2009, I was in high school in Kathmandu. The Maoists had called a 19-day blockade, and the city was at a standstill. A group of us - young, idealistic, maybe a little reckless - decided to use the time. We organized street debates for students in Baneshwor (infront of the Parliament). We thought: if people are stuck at home anyway, let's think together. Let's talk. The Maoists told us we could not debate on the streets. I remember what fear feels like in your body when you don't know if a threat is just a threat. We kept going and moved from the main road to the side streets. And then, eventually, we stopped. Last September, in 2025, high school students were back on those same streets in Baneshwor. They were the age I was in 2009. They were protesting. On September 8th, police fired on them. Nineteen young people were killed that day - and across the two days of protests and the unrest that followed, 76 people died in total. Those deaths broke something open in Nepal. They are the reason a 35-year-old prime minister was being sworn in yesterday at 12:34. The new parliament, the fresh faces, the average age of politicians cut nearly in half - all of it sits on top of kids who stood where I once stood and didn't come home. I have to hold that. I don't get to celebrate without holding that.

Here is the thing I haven't wanted to say out loud, but I'm going to say it now: My generation - the ones who debated on those streets in 2009 - we were supposed to be the ones. We were sharp, we were angry, we cared. And most of us took our scholarships and our job offers and we left. We are scattered across cities that are not Kathmandu, living lives made possible by the very privilege we once wanted to dismantle. I am one of them. We told ourselves we were ahead of our time. That Nepal wasn't ready. That we would find other ways to contribute. But I wonder, sometimes, whether we just found more comfortable ways to live. I am not trying to perform guilt here. Guilt without action is just another form of self-indulgence. But I think honesty requires me to say: we left, and the kids who couldn't leave stood on our streets, and some of them died, and now we are watching a ceremony on our phones and feeling moved. What does that mean? What do we owe?

Barack Obama, sitting with Anthony Bourdain over beer and bún chả in Hanoi, was asked: are we going to be okay? He said progress is never a straight line. I believe that. Nepal in March 2026 is genuinely better than Nepal in August 2025, which was better than Nepal in April 2005 during the civil war. In 2006 we began dismantling a 250-year-old monarchy - and by 2008 it was formally abolished, Nepal declared a republic. We wrote our own constitution in 2015. And now this. Progress is real. It just doesn't move the way we want it to, and it doesn't come free. My cousin's "let's see what will happen" is not wrong. It is wise, actually. The election was the beginning of something, not the resolution of it. These new leaders - young, promising, inheriting an enormous mess - will need to be watched, supported, pressured, held to account. Voting was the easy part. But I also know that I cannot let his caution become my permission to stay comfortable. The question isn't whether change is possible. We have seen it happen. The question is what I am willing to put on the line for it now, from where I am, with what I have.

If we (human civilization) are going to be around for another ten thousand years - and I believe we are- then each of us gets a small window. A few decades to push something forward, even slightly. The students in Baneshwor used their window. They didn't choose to, exactly. But they did. I am still choosing. So are you. And if you are diaspora, if you left, if you told yourself you'd find another way - I'm not here to make you feel bad. I'm here to say that I think we both know there is more we can do. Work with these new leaders. Stay on them. Don't let this be another cycle of hope followed by comfortable distance. We didn't do enough in 2009. I think we both know that. The question is what we do with the time we still have.
